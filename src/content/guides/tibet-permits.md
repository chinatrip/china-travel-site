# Tibet Travel Permits and Altitude Health

## Who needs a Tibet Travel Permit?

All foreign passport holders require a Tibet Travel Permit (TTT) to enter Tibet. This includes:
- All nationalities entering on a standard China tourist visa (L visa) or visa-free
- Taiwan residents with MTP (Mainland Travel Permit) documents

**Exceptions** (no TTT required):
- Hong Kong and Macau residents with Home Return Permits
- Chinese mainland citizens with national ID cards

Note: HK/Macau residents visiting Everest Base Camp still need a Frontier Certificate.

## What is the Tibet Travel Permit?

The TTT is a **paper document** listing the traveler's name, approved travel dates, and approved destinations within Tibet. It:
- Cannot be reused for subsequent trips
- Must be returned to the Tibet Tourism Bureau after your visit
- Is checked at all transportation entry points — train, plane, and road checkpoints

## How do I get a Tibet Travel Permit?

**Independent applications are not permitted.** Process:

1. Book a Tibet tour through a **licensed Chinese travel agency** — the agency submits the permit application on your behalf
2. Hold a valid China visa or qualify for visa-free entry (78 countries as of 2026 — see [[wiki/concepts/visa-types.md]])
3. Submit passport copy + China visa / entry-record copy to the agency
4. Allow **at least 10 working days** before your travel date (processing takes 5–7 working days)
5. Train passengers receive a permit copy; those flying to Lhasa need the **original permit**

**Cost:** the permit itself is free. The unavoidable cost is the tour package you must book through a licensed agency. Tour packages start around **USD $450–$2,090** depending on duration and destinations.

## Can foreigners travel independently in Tibet?

Foreign visitors cannot travel independently in Tibet:
- A **licensed Tibetan guide must accompany you throughout your stay**
- Self-drive and cycling tours are permitted but require a local guide and support vehicle at all times
- Unguided movement outside approved areas is not permitted

This is legally distinct from mainland China, where independent foreign travel is generally unrestricted — see [[wiki/concepts/trip-planning.md]].

## When is Tibet closed to foreign tourists?

In many past years Tibet closed to foreign visitors from **late February through end of March**, reopening around April 1, with no permits processed for travel inside that window (applications for April+ travel continued). This was an administrative pattern, not a fixed law, and it does not apply every year.

**2026 specific:** as of June 2026, multiple Tibet travel agencies report that — unlike the usual pattern — no closure occurred this year. gototibet.com (accessed June 2026) says "in March 2026, the travel restrictions were lifted, allowing foreign travelers to obtain Tibet Travel Permits and visit Tibet during this period," and The Land of Snows (accessed June 2026) states "in 2026, Tibet will remain open for all international travelers," including the Tibetan New Year (Losar, February 18, 2026) period and March. These are commercial agency sources rather than an official notice, and closures are set administratively at short notice — so confirm the current status with your booking agency before finalizing transport.

Because closure dates are set administratively and can change year to year, always confirm the current closure status with your agency before booking transport. Avoid October 1–7 (National Day Golden Week) — travel surges affect transport and accommodation throughout China during this period.

## Which areas of Tibet are restricted?

Even with a standard TTT, the following are off-limits to foreign visitors:
- Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon
- Lhamo La Tso (sacred lake)
- Yadong County
- Chamdo (eastern Tibet)
- Sites where sky burials occur

Parts of Tibet lie inside China's **border management areas** (边境管理区) — the controlled zones that run along the country's land frontiers, which also cover sections of Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, and Yunnan. Entering these zones requires a **Border Management Area Permit (BMAP, 边境管理区通行证)**.

**The BMAP is not the Tibet Travel Permit.** The TTT (above) governs tourist entry to the Tibet Autonomous Region; the BMAP governs access to border-control zones along the land frontier. They are separate documents with separate rules — one is not a substitute for the other, so don't confuse them.

As of **April 15, 2026** (announced April 2, 2026), the BMAP moved to an **electronic** format, and paper permits stopped being issued on that same date. Paper permits issued before then stay valid until they expire. Mainland Chinese residents aged 16 and over can now apply **online** — through the NIA 12367 app or the WeChat / Alipay mini-programs — for a permit valid up to three months.

**What this means for foreign travelers:** the online route is only for Chinese mainland residents aged 16 and over. Foreign nationals — together with Hong Kong / Macau / Taiwan residents, overseas Chinese, Chinese mainland residents traveling with a child under 16, and Chinese mainland residents applying for a one-year-validity permit — must apply **in person at a county-level (or higher) public-security exit-entry office or a designated police station**. The official notice sets out no online option and no first-versus-renewal shortcut for these groups, so the electronic rollout does not remove the in-person step for foreigners. Because foreigners cannot travel Tibet's restricted border zones independently, coordinate any such permit through your registered agency and guide — the same channel that arranges your Tibet Travel Permit.

## How do I enter Tibet from Nepal?

Travelers entering via Nepal (the Friendship Highway route) **cannot use a standard China visa**. They must obtain a **China Group Visa** from the Chinese Embassy in Kathmandu. Individual visas are not accepted for this entry route.

**Treat this route as unreliable rather than merely currently closed.** The Gyirong
(吉隆) crossing — the working Nepal land border since Zhangmu closed — has been
interrupted by natural disaster twice in fourteen months:

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 8 July 2025 | Flooding on the Dongling Zangbu destroyed the Resuo / Friendship Bridge, halting cross-border passenger and freight movement |
| 1 January 2026 | Port resumed passenger and freight clearance |
| 26 August 2026 | Mudslide on the Nepal side; Shigatse BMAP issuance suspended from 28 August (see above), no resumption announced as of 18 September 2026 |

Interrupted twice in fourteen months, then — roughly 177 days closed from the 2025
flood to the January reopening, about 237 days open after it, and closed again since
26 August. The point is the recurrence, not a majority of days: a reader told only
"currently suspended" will assume it is routine again next month, and the record does
not support that assumption either way. If your
itinerary depends on entering or leaving Tibet overland via Nepal, confirm the port's
current status with your agency **before** committing to flights, and keep a fly-in
alternative. The 2025–26 dates above come from Tibet Autonomous Region government
announcements; note that those sites blocked automated retrieval when this page was
last checked, so the port's day-to-day status could not be verified directly — ask
your agency rather than relying on this page for it.

## Altitude Sickness

Tibet is at extreme elevation — Lhasa alone is 3,650 m (12,000 ft), roughly 1,200 m higher than the highest ski resorts in the Alps. Most visitors experience some symptoms within 6–12 hours of arrival.

| Location | Elevation |
|---|---|
| Lhasa | 3,650 m / 11,975 ft |
| Shigatse | 3,840 m / 12,595 ft |
| Everest Base Camp (Tibet side) | 5,200 m / 17,060 ft |

For reference: Shangri-La (Yunnan) is 3,200 m / 10,500 ft; Xining (Qinghai) is 2,275 m / 7,464 ft and often used for acclimatization before Tibet.

### Symptoms of Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS)

Onset typically 6–12 hours after reaching altitude:
- Headache (most common; dull pressure sensation)
- Nausea and vomiting
- Fatigue and weakness
- Dizziness
- Shortness of breath at rest

Severe AMS can progress to:
- **HACE (High Altitude Cerebral Edema):** confusion, loss of coordination — life-threatening
- **HAPE (High Altitude Pulmonary Edema):** extreme breathlessness, pink frothy cough — life-threatening

Both HACE and HAPE require **immediate descent**. Do not wait until morning.

### Prevention

**Acclimatize:** arrive in Lhasa and rest for 1–2 full days before traveling to higher elevations (Shigatse, EBC). Do not fly from a low-altitude city and hike the same day. *(EBC — not Shigatse city — sits inside the border management area whose permit is currently suspended; see the suspension notice under Restricted Areas before planning this leg.)*

**Hydrate:** 3–4 litres of water per day at altitude; avoid alcohol for the first 2–3 days.

**Move slowly:** no vigorous physical activity for the first 48 hours.

**Rhodiola rosea (红景天):** traditional Chinese herbal supplement. Start 1–2 weeks before arrival. Available at pharmacies throughout China and in Lhasa. Evidence is mixed but widely used with no known safety concerns.

**Acetazolamide (Diamox):** prescription diuretic that speeds acclimatization. Dose: 125–250 mg twice daily, starting 1–2 days before ascent. Common side effects: increased urination, tingling in hands/feet, altered taste in carbonated drinks. Obtain a prescription before travel — declare to Chinese customs if carrying more than a 7-day supply.

### What to Do in Lhasa

- Oxygen canisters available at most Lhasa hotels, often free or for a small fee
- Pharmacies in Lhasa carry basic AMS medications
- If severe symptoms develop (confusion, unable to walk straight, extreme breathlessness at rest): **descend immediately**

## What travel insurance do I need for Tibet?

Standard travel insurance frequently excludes Tibet and high-altitude emergencies. Verify your policy covers:
- Medical treatment at altitude
- **Helicopter or air evacuation** — ground ambulance from EBC to Lhasa takes 6+ hours; helicopter is the only practical emergency option
- Tibet (and Xinjiang) are not excluded as conflict/restricted zones
